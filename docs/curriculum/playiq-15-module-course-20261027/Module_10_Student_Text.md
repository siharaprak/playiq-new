# PlayIQ Module 10: Test Your Tutor and Learn With It

## Your next unlock: put your new Tutor to work

Would you like to see if the Tutor you built can help with something new?
Would you want to know if it asks before guessing and protects your own
thinking? That is exactly what today's test drive is for.

Your Tutor is built. Now comes the exciting part: you get to see what it
actually does with a real question. Does it help you find the next step? Does
it ask before guessing? Does it help you understand instead of doing the
thinking for you?

This module keeps the Tutor build in Module 9 intact and gives it a full test
drive here. You will test six important behaviors, improve one weak spot, and
practice a repeatable way to use your Tutor whenever a new idea catches your
attention.

**Your route:** open the same Tutor Project, run the tests one at a time, make
one evidence-based improvement, then try the learning loop with a topic that
is new to you.

**By the end of this module, I can test my Tutor, make one careful improvement,
and use it to explore a new topic while keeping my own thinking active.**

**Skill Tree:** Tutor Test Pilot  
**Mission:** Test, improve, and use the Tutor's learning loop.

## Part 0: The 30-second Tutor launch

Imagine you ask your Tutor, “Can you teach me something I have never learned
before?” What would make its first reply useful?

**A.** It immediately gives you a long explanation.

**B.** It asks what you already know and what you want to be able to do.

Circle: **A / B**

There is no score here. The point is to notice that a useful Tutor starts by
understanding the learning mission before it launches into an answer.

## Part 2: Six quick tests for your Tutor

After each reply, write what the Tutor actually did. Do not mark a test as
successful just because the answer sounded confident.

Use this simple rating:

- **3 — Worked:** it did the useful behavior and stayed within your rules.
- **2 — Almost:** the main idea was right, but one part needs improvement.
- **1 — Missed:** it skipped the behavior or crossed a boundary.
- **N/T — Not tested:** you did not run it. Do not guess a score.

## Part 3: Improve one behavior, then retest it

Choose the weakest result that matters to you. Do not rewrite every file at
once. A small change makes it easier to tell what helped.

1. Describe the behavior you saw, without guessing why it happened.
2. Find the instruction connected to that behavior.
3. Write one clear change. For example: “After giving one hint, wait for my
   attempt before giving another.”
4. Ask your parent to review the change if it changes a Project rule or file.
5. Apply the change yourself in the Tutor Project.
6. Run the same test again. Record both attempts, even if the second one is
   still not right.

## Part 4: The repeatable “new idea to learning” loop

Your Tutor is not only for a topic assigned in school. You can use the same
careful learning loop when you become curious about a new subject, skill, or
idea. The Tutor can help you get started, but you still choose the goal, do
the practice, and check what you can explain or apply.

### Use the loop, step by step

1. **Name the idea.** Write the question or skill you want to explore.
2. **Set a small finish line.** For example: “I can explain the main idea in
   my own words,” or “I can complete one beginner example.”
3. **Show what you already know.** Ask the Tutor to ask you one question at
   a time. It should not turn an early guess into a permanent label.
4. **Choose a starting format.** Ask for a short explanation, a diagram,
   spoken-style explanation, worked example, hands-on plan, or a mix. The
   format you like is a useful starting preference, not proof that you can
   only learn one way.
5. **Try it yourself.** Explain, solve, build, draw, or retrieve the idea
   without looking at the answer.
6. **Check and adjust.** Ask the Tutor to check your attempt. If you are still
   confused, identify the missing connection and try a different explanation
   or example.
7. **Save a short learning note.** Record the topic, what you tried, what you
   could do afterward, and what you want to test next.

### Copy-and-paste learning launch

> I want to learn about [IDEA] so I can [SMALL GOAL]. First ask what I know
> and what I am curious about, one question at a time. Then suggest one short
> first explanation and one way I can try the idea myself. Let me choose the
> format. After I try, check my explanation or attempt and help me find one
> next step. Do not turn my preferences into a fixed label or claim I have
> mastered something unless my attempt shows it.

### A clear note about “learning styles”

People can have preferences, strengths, and different starting knowledge.
But saying “I prefer videos” does not by itself prove that videos will always
teach every subject best. Research on matching instruction to fixed style
labels has not established a dependable rule for every learner or topic.
That is why this course teaches you to ask, try, remember, explain, and check
what worked. Keep your Tutor's notes as **changeable ideas to test**, not a
permanent diagnosis or a promise that one format will always make learning
faster.

The useful question is not “What type of learner am I forever?” It is:

> “For this topic and this goal, which approach helped me understand and show
> what I know—and what should I try next?”

## Quick Quiz: how do you know it worked?

1. A test you did not run should be recorded as:
   - **A.** Passed, because the Tutor was designed carefully.
   - **B.** `not_tested`.
2. If one Tutor behavior misses, what should you change first?
   - **A.** One related instruction, then repeat the same test.
   - **B.** Rewrite every file at once.
3. Does liking a video prove that video always teaches every subject best?
   - **A.** Yes.
   - **B.** No. It is a preference to test against what you can explain or do.

**Answer key:** 1-B, 2-A, 3-B.
