## The Genie Principle

You have probably heard stories about a magical genie in a bottle. If you were handed three wishes right now, what would you ask for?

Here is the secret of artificial intelligence: **The prompt is part of the wish.**

If you ask a genie a vague, sloppy wish like *"make me rich"*, you might end up with a pile of useless Confederate currency or buried under a mountain of heavy gold coins you cannot move. 

AI works the exact same way. If you give an AI a lazy prompt like *"help me with my homework"*, it guesses what you need. Usually, it spits out a bland, robotic essay or a solved math equation that teaches you nothing and sounds like a machine wrote it.

```text
       ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
       │                   THE GENIE PRINCIPLE                   │
       ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
       │  Lazy Wish     ➔ Sloppy Guess    ➔ Zero Real Learning   │
       │  Clear Prompt  ➔ Socratic Coach  ➔ Permanent Skill Gain │
       └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

There is no shortcut to real knowledge. But there are infinitely better paths to learning it.

In PlayIQ, your teaching sequence is always:
1. **Imagine:** Identify the wish, problem, concept, or curiosity in your head.
2. **Clarify:** Write a sharp prompt that sets the rules of engagement.
3. **Engage:** Use Orion to explain, question, hint, quiz, and diagnose confusion.
4. **Verify:** Test whether the AI's answer is accurate, logical, and in your voice.
5. **Build:** Turn the knowledge into a physical build, an essay, a code project, or an artifact you own.

### The Core Architecture: System Prompt vs. User Prompt

To command an AI, you must understand how frontier models actually work under the hood:

```text
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                        HOW AI MEMORY IS SPLIT                          │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  SYSTEM INSTRUCTIONS (The "House Rules" / DNA)                        │
│  - Lives in background settings or project instructions.               │
│  - Never forgets. Follows these rules in every single chat.            │
│  - Controls: Tone, Role, Guardrails, and How it Explains.              │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  USER PROMPT (The "Daily Visitor" / Task)                              │
│  - What you type in the text box today.                                │
│  - Examples: "Explain question 4", "Give me a hint on this equation". │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

When people complain that AI *"is too verbose"*, *"gives away the answers"*, or *"hallucinates"*, it is because their AI has **no System Instructions**. It is running on default factory mode.

## Part 2 — Node 2: Choosing the Right AI Mode

Different learning bottlenecks require different kinds of coaching. If you use the wrong mode, studying becomes sluggish and irritating. When you match the mode to the bottleneck, understanding clicks in minutes.

```text
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤

│ 6. LESSON RESCUE MODE | When an entire chapter feels hopelessly lost.  │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Deep Dive: How to Trigger Each Mode

#### 1. Explain Mode
* **When to use:** You have never seen the material before, or the teacher's lecture flew over your head.
* **Master Prompt:**  
  > *"Explain [Concept] using one concrete real-world analogy. Keep it under three short paragraphs, then ask me one question to see what I understood."*

#### 2. Hint Mode
* **When to use:** You understand the basics, you have pencil on paper, but you are stuck on step 3.
* **Master Prompt:**  
  > *"Give me ONE hint only for this problem. Do not show the calculation or solve it. Wait for my attempt."*

#### 3. Quiz Mode
* **When to use:** You just read your notes and want to prove you actually know the material (retrieval practice).
* **Master Prompt:**  
  > *"Quiz me on [Topic] one question at a time. Mix multiple-choice and short-answer. Do not reveal the answer until I submit my guess."*

#### 4. Coach Mode
* **When to use:** You have 3 assignments due tomorrow, your backpack is messy, and you feel paralyzed by overwhelm.
* **Master Prompt:**  
  > *"Help me divide these 3 tasks into three 25-minute focused work blocks with 5-minute breaks. I will do all the work; you provide the pacing."*

#### 5. Learn-My-Way Mode
* **When to use:** Traditional textbook definitions feel dry, robotic, or abstract.
* **Master Prompt:**  
  > *"Teach me [Concept] starting with [my preferred style: e.g. a video game analogy / a visual schematic / a story], then connect it to the formal definition."*

#### 6. Lesson Rescue Mode
* **When to use:** You look at a paragraph or math problem and have no idea where to even start.
* **Master Prompt:**  
  > *"Ask me three short diagnostic questions to locate the exact step where my understanding broke. Rescue only that step."*

## Part 4 — Node 4: The PlayIQ Verification Ritual

## Part 1: Why Pre-Learning Destroys Classroom Overwhelm

### Cognitive Load and Working Memory

Your brain's working memory can only hold about 4 to 7 new pieces of information at once. When a teacher introduces 15 new terms, 3 formulas, and a historical timeline all in 45 minutes, your working memory crashes like a computer trying to run 50 apps at once. Psychologists call this **cognitive overload**.

```
WITHOUT PRE-LEARNING:
Teacher speaks ---> 15 new terms hit your brain at once ---> Buffer overflow ---> Overwhelm & zoned out

WITH PRE-LEARNING:
5-min Pre-Learn Map ---> 5 landmarks already saved in long-term memory ---> Teacher speaks ---> New details easily snap into place!
```

### The 5-Part Map

A high-speed Pre-Learn Map does not summarize every paragraph. It answers exactly five questions:

1. **The Headline:** What is the overarching topic in one sentence?
2. **The 5 Landmarks:** What are the 4 to 5 main parts or phases?
3. **The Power Vocabulary:** Which 3 to 5 critical terms will the teacher repeat most?
4. **The Anchor Example:** What is one real-world, everyday example that illustrates the concept?
5. **The Golden Question:** What is the single most interesting question to listen for in class?

### Real-World Models Across Three Subjects

Look at how a 5-Part Map simplifies three notoriously difficult subjects:

#### Model 1: Biology — Cellular Respiration
* **Headline:** How living cells break down glucose (food) to create usable energy (ATP).
* **5 Landmarks:** 
  1. Glycolysis (splitting sugar in the cytoplasm)
  2. Krebs Cycle (spinning carbon rings inside the mitochondria)
  3. Electron Transport Chain (the cellular power generator)
  4. ATP (the energy battery produced)
  5. Byproducts (carbon dioxide and water)
* **Power Vocabulary:** Glucose, ATP, Mitochondria, Aerobic, Byproduct.
* **Anchor Example:** Why your leg muscles burn during a sprint (when oxygen runs low, cells switch to lactic acid fermentation).
* **Golden Question:** *"Where exactly inside the mitochondria is the majority of ATP created?"*

#### Model 2: World History — Causes of World War I
* **Headline:** How decades of competing treaties, empires, and military buildups exploded into a global war after a single assassination.
* **5 Landmarks:**
  1. Militarism (arms race between navies and armies)
  2. Alliances (secret defense pacts that dragged countries in like dominoes)
  3. Imperialism (fights over colonies in Africa and Asia)
  4. Nationalism (intense ethnic pride and desire for self-rule)
  5. The Spark (the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo)
* **Power Vocabulary:** Alliance, Mobilization, Deterrence, Sovereignty, Domino Effect.
* **Anchor Example:** Two rival friend groups in a school hallway where a conflict between two people suddenly pulls 30 people into a fight because of pacts.
* **Golden Question:** *"Why did the alliances force countries to declare war even when their leaders didn't want to fight?"*

#### Model 3: Algebra II — Logarithms
* **Headline:** The mathematical inverse of exponents—asking *"what power do I need to reach this number?"*
* **5 Landmarks:**
  1. Base (the number being multiplied)
  2. Exponent/Power (how many times it multiplies)
  3. Result/Argument (the final total)
  4. Common Log (base 10)
  5. Natural Log ($\ln$, base $e$)
* **Power Vocabulary:** Base, Exponent, Inverse, Argument, Logarithmic Scale.
* **Anchor Example:** The Richter scale for earthquakes (a magnitude 7 earthquake isn't 1 point stronger than a 6; it's 10 times stronger).
* **Golden Question:** *"Why can you never take the logarithm of a negative number?"*

## Part 4: Non-Examples Reveal the Boundary

One of the biggest traps in learning is **false recognition**—thinking you understand a concept when you only recognize the label.

The fastest way to test true understanding is by finding the **Non-Example**.
- An **Example** shows what a concept is.
- A **Non-Example** looks almost identical on the surface, but violates one critical rule of the concept.

Seeing where a concept *stops* working is what builds true expertise.

### Model: The Boundary Detective

| Concept | True Example | Non-Example (Close Call!) | Why It Fails the Rule (The Boundary) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Direct Democracy** | Citizens in ancient Athens voting directly on a proposed law in the assembly. | Citizens in Canada voting for a Member of Parliament to represent them in the House of Commons. | Canada is a *Representative* Democracy; citizens elect representatives rather than voting directly on the laws themselves. |
| **Chemical Change** | An iron nail rusting in moist air to form iron oxide. | Ice melting into liquid water on a warm kitchen counter. | Melting ice is a *Physical* Change; the water molecules ($H_2O$) remain chemically identical. |
| **Alliteration** | "Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers." | "City life is simple and serene." | Even though 'city', 'simple', and 'serene' start with an 's' sound, alliteration requires the repetition of initial consonant sounds across stressed syllables. |

## Your mission: find the one missing piece

Have you ever looked at a lesson and thought, "I understand every word, but I
still do not understand what this means"?

That feeling does not mean you are bad at the subject. It usually means there
is a specific missing piece hiding inside the lesson. In this module, you will
learn how to find that piece and ask Orion for the right kind of help.

By the end of this module, I can:

- turn "I do not get it" into a precise diagnosis;
- recognize six common kinds of learning gaps;
- use chunking to locate where confusion begins;
- compare three rescue methods on the same real problem;
- choose a rescue setting for Orion to test, not a permanent label;
- teach back a rescued lesson in my own words;
- add evidence from this module to my future personal tutor Project.

> **ORION:** "Confusion is not a dead end. It is a signal. If we can find the
> exact point where the signal began, we can choose a smaller and smarter next
> move. Ready to turn confusion into a map?"

## Part 1: Confusion is information

When you are stuck, your brain may want to escape, reread everything, or ask
for the complete answer. Those reactions are normal, but they do not always
repair the problem.

Lesson Rescue Mode follows four moves:

1. **Stop** at the first unclear point.
2. **Name** the likely gap.
3. **Choose** one rescue method.
4. **Prove** the rescue by explaining or using the idea yourself.

This is not about making every lesson instantly easy. It is about reducing a
large confusing problem into the next learnable step.

### Orion conversation: from fog to a target

**STUDENT:** "I do not understand this chapter."

**ORION:** "Let us make that smaller. Which paragraph is the first one that
stops making sense?"

**STUDENT:** "The second paragraph."

**ORION:** "Which word, step, or connection inside it is unclear?"

**STUDENT:** "I know the words, but I do not know why the equation changes on
line three."

**ORION:** "Good diagnosis. That sounds like a possible step gap. I will show
only the move between lines two and three, then you will try a similar move."

Notice what happened: Orion did not replace the student's thinking or finish
the whole assignment. Orion helped identify the missing step and then returned
the work to the student.

---

## Part 2: The six gap types

The same confused feeling can come from different causes. Use these six types
as possibilities, not medical diagnoses or permanent traits.

### 1. Word gap

An important word, symbol, phrase, or instruction is unclear.

Example: You cannot follow a history question because you do not know what
`evaluate` means in that question.

Useful Orion request:

> "In this sentence, explain the word `evaluate` in plain language. Give one
> example of what it asks me to do and one thing it does not ask me to do."

### 2. Background gap

The lesson assumes you already know an earlier idea.

Example: Factoring quadratics feels impossible because multiplying binomials
was never fully understood.

Useful Orion request:

> "Ask me three short questions to find the earlier idea I may be missing. Do
> not solve the current problem for me."

### 3. Step gap

You understand the starting point and maybe the ending, but one move in the
middle is missing.

Example: You follow the first two steps in a chemistry calculation and then do
not know why the units change.

Useful Orion request:

> "Show only the missing move between these two lines. Explain why that move is
> allowed, then give me a similar move to try."

### 4. Connection gap

You understand separate facts but not how they influence one another.

Example: You know what supply and demand mean separately, but not why a shortage
can affect price.

Useful Orion request:

> "Connect these two ideas using one cause-and-effect chain and one real-world
> example. Then ask me to explain the connection back."

### 5. Overload gap

Too much information is arriving at once, so you cannot see where to begin.

Example: A page contains seven new terms, two diagrams, and a long explanation.

Useful Orion request:

> "Break this into no more than three chunks. Tell me which chunk to understand
> first and hide the later details until I ask for them."

### 6. Expectation gap

You may understand the topic but not what the task, teacher, rubric, or question
expects you to produce.

Example: You know the novel, but you are unsure whether the assignment asks for
a summary, an argument, or evidence-based analysis.

Useful Orion request:

> "Translate this instruction into a checklist. Do not write the response.
> Show me what a successful answer must demonstrate."

## Part 3: Chunking—the confusion scanner

Chunking means dividing material into small enough pieces that you can locate
the first breakdown.

### The line-by-line scan

Use a short, school-approved paragraph or worked example. Do not upload private
information or another student's work.

1. Read only the first sentence or step.
2. Mark it:
   - `G` = I get it well enough to explain it.
   - `M` = maybe; I recognize it but cannot explain it yet.
   - `S` = stop; this is where I lose the thread.
3. Continue only until the first `M` or `S`.
4. Underline the exact word, step, or connection causing the uncertainty.
5. Name a possible gap type.

# PlayIQ Module 5: Compression Learning

## Your mission: make a big idea small without breaking it

Imagine being able to look at a crowded chapter, a long video, or a page of
notes and pull out the few ideas that actually hold everything together.

That is compression. It is not shrinking words just to make a shorter page. It
is building a clear mental map that keeps the truth, removes clutter, and helps
you remember what matters.

By the end of this module, I can:

- separate a topic's core idea from its supporting details;
- simplify an explanation without making it inaccurate;
- explain one idea in three useful ways;
- build a one-page Understanding Card;
- compare audio review, a visual card, and teach-back fairly;
- choose a memory-review setting for Orion to test;
- create evidence and a knowledge-file record for my personal tutor Project.

> **ORION:** "A powerful question is not always longer. Sometimes it removes
> everything that does not matter yet. Today, you will learn how to fold a big
> topic into a map small enough to carry—and accurate enough to trust."

---

## Part 1: Compression is not deletion

A useful compression answers three questions:

1. What is the central idea?
2. Which details are necessary to understand it?
3. What would become false or misleading if removed?

### Weak compression

> "Photosynthesis is plants eating sunlight."

It is memorable, but it is inaccurate. Plants do not eat sunlight. The phrase
also removes water, carbon dioxide, and the creation of sugar.

### Safer compression

> "Photosynthesis is how plants use light energy, water, and carbon dioxide to
> make sugar, releasing oxygen as part of the process."

This version is still simple, but it preserves the major ingredients, action,
and result.

### Orion's truth check

**STUDENT:** "Can you make this as short as possible?"

**ORION:** "I can make it shorter, but first tell me what you need it for. A
one-line preview, a test explanation, and a lab report require different levels
of detail."

**STUDENT:** "I need a first map before studying the chapter."

**ORION:** "Then I will give you the core idea, three necessary parts, and one
warning about what the short version leaves out."

Why this matters: the purpose controls how much detail is safe to remove.

## Your mission: turn wrong answers into your fastest study map

What if every mistake could tell you exactly what to learn next?

## Part 1: Rereading can feel easier than remembering

When the answer is visible, your brain can recognize it. Recognition feels
familiar, but a test usually asks you to produce, explain, select, or use the
idea without the answer sitting in front of you.

Self-testing closes that gap:

1. Hide the source.
2. Try to retrieve or use the idea.
3. Check against an approved source.
4. Investigate the mistake.
5. Correct it in your own words.
6. Try a new question that tests the same missing skill.

### Orion conversation: coach, not answer machine

**STUDENT:** "I got it wrong. Just tell me the answer."

**ORION:** "I will help, but first let us use the mistake. Was the problem a
missing fact, an unclear word, a skipped step, a rushed error, difficulty using
the idea in a new situation, or confidence that did not match the answer?"

**STUDENT:** "I knew the rule, but I applied it to the wrong number."

**ORION:** "That may be a step or attention error rather than missing knowledge.
Show me the line where you chose the number. Then I will give a hint and a
similar question so you can prove the correction."

Why this matters: the same wrong answer can need very different repairs. More
rereading will not fix every kind of mistake.

---

## Part 2: The six Mistake Bank categories

Categories are working hypotheses. You can revise one after looking more
closely.

### 1. Knowledge gap

You did not yet know or remember an important fact, rule, or concept.

Example: You cannot explain the purpose of mitochondria because the function
was never learned clearly.

Useful repair: return to the core explanation, then retrieve it again.

### 2. Word gap

An important word, symbol, or instruction was misunderstood.

Example: You answer only one side of a question because you misread `contrast`.

Useful repair: define the term in context and compare an example with a
non-example.

### 3. Step error

You knew the idea but skipped, reversed, or misapplied a step.

Example: You forgot to distribute a negative sign through both terms.

Useful repair: identify the decision point, explain why the step is needed,
then try a matched example.

### 4. Careless or attention error

Your understanding may be present, but the response contains a copying,
reading, sign, unit, or checking mistake.

Example: You solve correctly but copy `42` as `24` in the final line.

Useful repair: create a final check that targets the exact repeated risk.

Do not use "careless" as an insult. It describes the event, not the person.

### 5. Transfer error

You understand the idea in a familiar example but do not yet recognize how to
use it in a different situation.

Example: You can identify a metaphor in a practice sentence but miss it inside
a poem.

Useful repair: compare the familiar and new cases, identify what stayed the
same, and practise one more varied example.

### 6. Confidence error

Your confidence and your demonstrated answer did not match.

Examples:

- You were certain about an incorrect answer.
- You had a correct explanation but changed it because you did not trust it.

Useful repair: record confidence before checking and learn which clues deserve
trust.

you, Orion, a parent, or a teacher trace an important claim instead of trusting

### 3. Source notes

Copy only short, permitted facts or write accurate notes from the source. Give
each fact a source label.

```text

- [S1] Another fact or note from the same source.
- [S2] A fact from a different approved source.
```

Why it matters: Orion can connect claims to sources and can tell you when the
file does not contain enough information to answer.

### 4. Ten-line summary

Write the first version yourself. Use no more than ten short lines.

A useful summary includes:

- the main idea;
- the most important parts or steps;
- one cause-and-effect connection;
- one example;
- one limit, exception, or uncertainty when relevant.

Why it matters: choosing what belongs in ten lines forces you to process the
topic. Copying an entire page saves words but may not build understanding.

### 5. Key terms

Choose five terms that unlock the topic.

```text
Term:
Meaning in my own words:
Source:
Example or connection:
```

Why it matters: one missing word can make an entire explanation feel harder
than it really is.

### 6. Questions for active recall

Turn the notes into questions that make you retrieve or use the information.

- **Easy:** checks a key fact or definition.
- **Medium:** explains a step, cause, or connection.
- **Challenge:** applies, compares, predicts, or solves.

Why it matters: notes become more useful when they can test you. Rereading can
feel familiar even when you cannot recall the idea without looking.

### 7. Missing links and Mistake Bank connection

Record what is not yet clear.

```text

Missing background fact:
Missing step:
Missing connection:
Related Mistake Bank entry:
Next question to investigate:
```

Why it matters: confusion becomes a target instead of a fog. Orion can help
with the missing piece rather than repeating the whole chapter.

### 8. Precision tutor prompt

Tell Orion how to use the file.

```text

Task: Quiz me on [topic] using only the approved Study Pack.
Limits: If the file does not support an answer, say "Not found in this file."
Format: Ask one easy, one medium, and one challenge question, one at a time.
Effort rule: Wait for my attempt. Give one hint before explaining.
Verification: Cite the source label connected to your explanation.
```

Why it matters: a file supplies the context; the prompt supplies the job and
boundaries.

## Part 1 - Clear writing makes thinking visible

A teacher cannot see the answer in your head. Clear writing creates a path the
reader can follow.

One useful path is **CER**:

- **Claim:** your answer or main point;
- **Evidence:** the fact, example, quotation, observation, or data that supports
  the claim;
- **Reasoning:** your explanation of how or why the evidence supports the
  claim.

### See CER in action

Question: Why did the covered cup of water stay cooler in the sun?

**Claim:** The cover helped the water stay cooler.

**Evidence:** After twenty minutes, the covered cup measured 3 degrees cooler
than the uncovered cup in the same location.

**Reasoning:** Because both cups were in the same location, the temperature
difference suggests that the cover reduced some of the direct heating from
sunlight.

Notice what the reasoning does. It does not merely repeat the evidence. It
explains the connection while avoiding a claim larger than the observation can
support.

### Quick CER check

Read this answer:

> School gardens can support science learning because students can observe
> plant growth over time. For example, they can measure how light or water
> changes growth. This gives students direct evidence they can compare with
> ideas from class.

Underline the claim. Box the evidence or example. Circle the reasoning.

### Why this activity matters

CER gives Orion something specific to coach. Instead of saying, "Make this
better," you can ask, "Is my evidence relevant?" or, "Did I explain the link
between my evidence and my claim?"

## Part 2 - Outline first so the blank page has fewer decisions

```text

```

### Example Answer Map

```text
Question or task: Why are pollinators important?
```

## Part 3 - Ask for coaching that leaves the decisions with you

Compare these requests.

**Replacement request**

> "Write my paragraph about sleep. Make it sound like me."

**Coaching request**

> "Act as my writing coach. Review my Answer Map for claim, evidence, and
> reasoning. Ask me one question about the weakest part. Then give up to three
> specific suggestions without writing any sentences for me."

The second request protects ownership because you provide the thinking and
Orion responds with questions and feedback.

### The PlayIQ writing-coach prompt

```text
Role: Act as my writing coach, not my ghostwriter.
Context: I will provide the task, my Answer Map, and my own draft.
Check: Focus on [claim | evidence | reasoning | organization | clarity].
Limits: Do not rewrite sentences or create a final answer for me.
Format: Ask one diagnostic question, then give up to three specific suggestions.
Voice rule: Point out words that sound generic or unlike my sample, but let me
choose the replacement.
Evidence rule: Identify unsupported claims and ask me for a source or a smaller
claim.
Effort rule: Wait for my revision before giving more feedback.
```

### What useful feedback sounds like

Useful coaching is specific and actionable:

- "Your claim answers the question, but the evidence is still general. What
  observation or source fact could you add?"
- "The reasoning repeats the evidence. Explain why that fact leads to your
  claim."
- "This phrase sounds more formal than the rest of your writing. Would you
  normally say it this way?"

Less useful feedback is vague or takes over:

- "Make it better."
- "This is bad."
- a replacement paragraph;
- a long list of edits with no explanation.

Why this matters: good feedback teaches you what to notice the next time. A
replacement answer may finish one task without improving your skill.

## Part 1: What makes a personal tutor Project different?

A normal chat starts with whatever you type in that moment. A Project can hold
instructions and approved files that give the AI useful context across your
work inside that Project.

Platform names and controls can change. Your parent should help you locate the
feature called **Project**, **workspace**, **custom assistant**, or the closest
approved equivalent. If the approved account does not offer Projects, complete
the files and tests in a regular private conversation. Do not create a new
account or purchase anything without your parent.

### The three layers of your tutor

**Layer 1 — Personality**

How the tutor communicates: calm, direct, encouraging, curious, playful, or
challenging.

**Layer 2 — Function**

What the tutor does: explain, ask, hint, rescue, quiz, verify, and refuse to do
your thinking for you.

**Layer 3 — Knowledge**

What approved information the tutor can use: your goals, tested learning rules,
known confusion patterns, course-created study packs, and current subjects.

Personality affects how the experience feels. Function affects whether the
tutor actually helps you learn. Knowledge gives the tutor relevant context.
You need all three, but function and safety come first.

### Why this activity matters

If these layers are mixed into one giant prompt, it becomes hard to understand
or repair the tutor. Separating them lets you answer three useful questions:

- Is the tone wrong?
- Is the teaching behavior wrong?
- Is the tutor missing information?

## Part 1: Tutor vs. assistant

Your tutor from Module 9 helps **you learn**. An assistant helps a **user do a
specific task**.

| Personal tutor | Real-user assistant |
|---|---|
| Main goal: strengthen learning | Main goal: support a defined task |
| Uses your approved learning evidence | Uses only approved task context |
| Questions, hints, quizzes, and rescues | Organizes, drafts, compares, checks, or guides |
| Must protect your thinking | Must protect user control and decisions |

An assistant is not useful because it sounds impressive. It is useful when the
real user can complete a task more clearly, safely, or conveniently.

### Assistant Purpose Formula

> This assistant helps **[user]** do **[task]** by **[support method]**, while
> avoiding **[boundary]**.

Example:

> This assistant helps my parent plan three school lunches by asking about
> ingredients and constraints, then drafting options, while avoiding medical
> advice, purchases, and assumptions about allergies.

### Why this matters

The formula prevents "build an assistant for everything." One user, one task,
one support method, and clear boundaries produce a prototype that can actually
be tested.

## Part 2: The Exam & Performance System

The older Exam & Performance System now lives inside the Master Trial. It is
the sequence you use to prepare, perform, and learn from the result.

### The six-stage performance loop

1. **Define** — understand what the performance actually requires.
2. **Map** — pre-learn the topic and locate the hard parts.
3. **Rescue** — repair missing connections.
4. **Practise** — retrieve, apply, explain, and use the Mistake Bank.
5. **Perform** — complete a timed or realistic attempt without hidden help.
6. **Review** — compare evidence and choose the next improvement.

This is not cheating. Preparation makes the real event feel more familiar.
The real advantage is learning how to learn sooner, with less wasted effort,
while still doing the thinking and practice yourself.

## Part 7: Build the Learning Dashboard

### Why this matters

A dashboard is not decoration and it is not a machine that declares how smart
you are. It organizes evidence so you can notice patterns and decide what to do
next.

Your dashboard may be a table, spreadsheet, sketch, presentation, or app
prototype. It must remain human-readable.

### The five questions your dashboard should answer

1. What have I studied or practised?
2. Where does checked performance match or disagree with my confidence?
3. Which mistakes or missing connections repeat?
4. Which learning methods helped under which conditions?
5. What should I practise next, and why?

### Required data record

```yaml
- date: [date]
  topic: [topic]
  activity: [what was attempted]
  source: [module, worksheet, approved test, or student reflection]
  confidence_before: [1-5 or not_recorded]
  checked_result: [actual result or not_checked]
  mistake_or_gap: [observation or none_observed]
  method_tested: [method]
  help_needed: [none | hint | several_hints | other]
  next_step: [specific practice]
  evidence_status: [observed | student_reported | experiment_result | missing]
```

Use `not_recorded`, `not_checked`, or `missing` when data does not exist. Do not
